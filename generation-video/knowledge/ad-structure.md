# Short video ad structure

## Hook in the first 1–2 seconds
Viewers decide whether to keep watching almost immediately, so the opening shot must stop the scroll. Open on the most striking image you have: a bold close-up, an unexpected angle, or the viewer's problem shown in one frame. Don't open on a logo or a slow establishing shot. The first narration line should be short and direct, like a question or a promise, not a company introduction.

## One product, one message
A short ad can land exactly one idea. Pick a single product (or offer) and a single message, and make every scene serve it. Cut secondary features, extra audiences and taglines that compete with the main point. If a scene doesn't support the one message, remove it.

## Show the benefit, not the features
Show what the customer's life looks like with the product (time saved, comfort, confidence, delight) rather than listing specs. Put a person in the frame experiencing the result. Say features only when they explain the benefit ("brews in 60 seconds, so mornings stay calm").

## Visual clarity for ad frames
Each frame should read in under a second. Use one clear subject, a clean uncluttered background, strong lighting that separates the subject from the background, and the subject on a rule-of-thirds line. Keep one consistent look (palette, lighting, lens) across scenes so the ad feels like one piece. Keep generated images free of text and logos, and put any words in the on-screen headline instead.

## One clear call to action at the end
End with a single, specific action: "Try it today", "Order now", "Visit the site". Give the CTA its own final scene with calm, uncluttered framing and the product as the hero, and hold it long enough to read (about 2 seconds or more). Never offer two competing actions.

## Pacing for 5, 10, 15 and 30-second ads
5s: two beats, a hook and then the product with the CTA. 10s: hook, product, one benefit, CTA (3–4 scenes of 2–3s). 15s: hook, product, two benefits, CTA (4–5 scenes). 30s: hook, product, two or three benefits, a proof moment, CTA (6–7 scenes of 4–5s). The hook takes about 15–20% of the runtime and the CTA about 20%. Change the shot every 2–4 seconds so the eye stays engaged.

## Narration tone and word budget
Write narration to be spoken at about 2.5 words per second, so a 2-second scene gets about 5 words. Use a confident, warm, conversational tone in the second person ("you"), with short sentences and active verbs. Headlines on screen stay at 6 words or fewer and support the narration without repeating it word for word. Don't make claims you can't support (prices, statistics, "best", awards).
