# Video production basics

Source: Craig Lebrau, "How Learning to Make Videos Will Add an Additional Skill-Set That Employers Love", SkillsYouNeed — https://www.skillsyouneed.com/rhubarb/making-videos-skillset.html (condensed; used with attribution per the site's terms).

## Why short video works
Video combines visuals, audio and narrative, so information is more digestible, easier to relate to and more memorable than dense text. A short, well-produced video is more engaging than a text-heavy explanation. Keep each clip focused on one message the viewer can take away.

## Plan before you generate: objective, audience, message
Great videos rarely happen by accident. Before generating anything, define the objective, who the audience is, and the single core message. A storyboard (a sequence of images representing the planned shots) makes the narrative clear and logical before rendering; written scene descriptions can be turned into visual panels automatically. When a request is vague, infer the objective and audience and write the prompt to serve that one message.

## Composition: rule of thirds and framing
Frame shots with the rule of thirds: place the subject on a third line or intersection rather than dead center, leaving breathing room in the direction the subject faces or moves. In image prompts, say where the subject sits in the frame (e.g. "subject on the left third, open space to the right") and the shot size (close-up, medium, wide).

## Lighting makes the biggest quality difference
Simple lighting changes dramatically improve footage, e.g. facing the subject toward a window for soft, even light. In prompts, always specify the light: its source, direction, softness and color temperature (e.g. "soft window light from the left, warm late-afternoon tone"). When editing a frame, keep the lighting consistent unless the user asks to change it.

## Stability: avoid shaky, distracting motion
Shaky footage looks unprofessional and distracts viewers; a tripod or any stable support fixes it. For generated clips, prefer steady, deliberate camera moves (locked-off, slow push in, slow pan) over handheld shake unless the user explicitly wants a handheld feel.

## Editing: select, sequence, trim — invisibly
Editing brings the story to life: select the best takes, arrange them in a logical sequence and trim unnecessary content. Use clean cuts and simple transitions, and layer text or graphics only to emphasize key points. Good editing is invisible: it guides the viewer's attention smoothly without drawing attention to itself. In a storyboard, each scene should earn its place; cut scenes that don't serve the core message.

## Audio matters more than image quality
Viewers forgive mediocre video but not bad audio; clear, crisp sound is instantly perceived as professional. Keep the voice clear and easy to understand with consistent levels. Background music should sit low to set the mood and never overpower narration or dialogue.

## Post-production polish: titles, lower thirds, color
Finish with an opening title and a closing card (credits or contact information). Simple on-screen graphics ("lower thirds") introduce a speaker or label a point professionally. Basic color correction (brightness and contrast) makes footage more vibrant and consistent across shots, so keep palette and exposure consistent between frames of the same video.

## Creativity and problem-solving under constraints
Production rarely goes exactly to plan; finding creative solutions to technical or narrative problems is a core skill. When a requested change is impossible or conflicts with the video's message, propose the closest achievable alternative instead of refusing.
